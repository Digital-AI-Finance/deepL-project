Succession structures are usually reviewed at a crisis, not on a schedule

linkedin_post · 224 words · published 2026-08-17 · risk tier T3

Succession structures are usually reviewed at a crisis rather than on a schedule.

The prompt is a death, an illness, a divorce, or a disagreement that has finally reached the point of needing a lawyer. The review then happens under time pressure, among people who are not speaking to each other, and with fewer options open than there were a year earlier.

The structure is rarely the thing that failed. A family foundation is established for a defined purpose set out in its founding documents, which govern how it may act. Those documents were often written for a family that met once a year in one country. Three decades on, the family may be spread across four, and the people named to make decisions may no longer be the people the family actually turns to. The document did what it was written to do. The family moved.

Nobody reviews a structure in a year when nothing is wrong. That is the year the question is cheapest to ask: does this still do what it was set up to do, and if not, what would changing it involve?

It is a question worth putting to whoever drafted the original documents, before there is a reason to ask it in a hurry. We work alongside a client's existing tax and legal advisers rather than replacing them.
