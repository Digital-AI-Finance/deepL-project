Relocating to Liechtenstein: the order in which things have to happen

blog · 1024 words · published 2026-08-17 · risk tier T3

A move to Liechtenstein usually arrives with a date attached — a start date, a school term, a lease that expires. What follows is a set of practical questions, and their order matters more than the pace at which they are answered. This piece sets out the ordinary sequence: what has to be settled before the move, what can safely follow it, and which steps are difficult to take back once taken.

The residence question sets the timetable

Every other date in a relocation hangs off the residence question, because it decides when the family can live in the country and on what basis.

Liechtenstein is a small jurisdiction, and the number of residence permits available to people without an employment link in the country is limited. The route that applies to a given family depends on nationality, on whether a Liechtenstein employer is involved, and on the composition of the household.

Two consequences follow. The first is that no general article can identify which route applies to a particular family. The second is that the outcome of a residence application is decided by the authorities that receive it, not by any adviser. Where anyone describes the result as settled before a decision has issued, they are describing something outside their control.

The first step, then, is not paperwork. It is establishing which route applies, where its decision point falls in the calendar, and how much of the rest of the move can sensibly be committed to before that point arrives.

Where local counsel is required rather than optional

Much of a relocation can be coordinated through a single point of contact. Some of it cannot be done by anyone other than a qualified practitioner in the destination jurisdiction, and treating those items as administrative is where timetables usually fail.

The residence filing, and anything that later amends it. Statements made in one filing are read against the ones that follow it. Consistency is worth more here than speed.

Employment, payroll and social insurance. Registration obligations attach to the employment relationship and to the date on which residence begins.

Residential property. The acquisition of residential property in Liechtenstein is subject to statutory restrictions and official approval. For most arriving families that makes renting first the practical route rather than a compromise.

Wills, marital property and succession documents. A change of habitual residence can change which country's law governs a will or a marital property regime. Checking that is counsel work in both the country being left and the country being entered.

Northwind's relocation support is coordinated with local counsel in the destination jurisdiction. The firm works alongside a client's existing tax and legal advisers rather than replacing them.

Before the move

Once the route is identified, the pre-move list is short and mostly unglamorous.

Housing sufficient to register an address, usually rented. Civil status documents — birth, marriage, records of previous residence — which often require certified translation or legalisation, and which are among the most common causes of a slipped date. School places, which run on an academic calendar nobody will move for you. Health insurance cover in place from the day residence begins.

And then the item that is regularly deferred and should not be: a review of the arrangements that the move itself will disturb. Existing wills and powers of attorney. Directorships held in the country being left. Any trust, foundation or holding company whose administration quietly assumes the family lives where it used to live. A change of residence can alter how existing arrangements operate before anyone has had the chance to look at them.

What can reasonably follow the move

More than most people expect. Banking and custody relationships, day-to-day administration, and the longer-horizon questions — succession, ownership of a family business, how the next generation participates — do not need to be resolved before a removal van is booked.

That work has its own timetable, and it is a longer one. Succession structures generally take longer to put in place than the transactions that prompt them, so the work usually begins well before a sale. A relocation is rarely the deadline for structural work. The permit is the deadline; the structure is the following year's conversation.

The steps that are hard to reverse

Four of them, in rough order of how often they cause trouble.

The date on which residence changes. It is a fact fixed at the time and awkward to reconstruct afterwards, and a good deal turns on it. Whether anything ought to happen before that date is a question for the family's own tax advisers, and the useful time to ask it is beforehand. The firm does not provide tax advice and does not structure arrangements by reference to their tax outcome.

Transfers made around the move. Gifts, and transfers of assets into or out of existing structures, are difficult to unwind once made.

Winding up an existing structure. A trust or foundation dissolved because it looked redundant cannot be reinstated on its original terms. Reviewing an old structure is reversible; closing it is not.

Statements made in filings. Immigration and civil registration files are read as a whole and across time. Correcting an earlier statement is possible, but it costs weeks the timetable may not have.

Where this leaves a family with a date in the diary

The honest summary is that only two things are genuinely urgent: identifying the residence route and its decision point, and looking once at the documents and structures the move will disturb. Everything else either follows from those or can wait until the family has arrived.

A short conversation about sequence — which route, which decision point, which items fall before it — is usually enough to see whether the timetable holds.
