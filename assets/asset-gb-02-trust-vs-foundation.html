Trust or foundation: where the discretion sits, and what happens when the family disagrees

blog · 1002 words · published 2026-08-17 · risk tier T3

A client of yours holds assets in three countries and has a family that does not agree about them. Someone has said the word "foundation". You know trusts well enough to explain one, and you would rather not learn Liechtenstein foundation practice in front of the client. What follows is the difference between the two as it shows up in practice: who holds the discretion, what each does at the moment the family falls out, and why the choice is usually settled by the family rather than by the tax position. It is general information only and does not constitute legal or tax advice.

Two different pieces of machinery

A professional trustee holds and administers trust assets for the beneficiaries under the terms of the trust deed. The trust is not a legal person. It is a relationship, and the trustee's legal ownership is the load-bearing part of it: beneficiaries hold rights against the trustee rather than against the assets directly.

A foundation inverts that. It is a legal person with its own assets and its own organs, and nobody owns it. A Liechtenstein foundation separates legal ownership of assets from the family that benefits from them. A family foundation is established for a defined purpose set out in its founding documents, which govern how it may act. The foundation council administers the assets in service of that purpose.

Relationship against legal person; duties owed to beneficiaries against a purpose to be served. Almost every practical difference below follows from that one.

Where the discretion sits

In a discretionary trust the discretion belongs to the trustee and is exercised towards a class of beneficiaries. A settlor's letter of wishes informs that discretion without binding it. The trustee's reasoning is tested, when it is tested, against the duties owed to those beneficiaries, and in most trust jurisdictions a beneficiary can put the exercise of a discretion before a court.

In a foundation, the discretion belongs to the council and is bounded first by purpose. The founder's intentions are not a side letter; they sit in the constitutive documents, and altering them afterwards is a formal act rather than a change of mind.

The trade is a real one, and it is worth putting to your client in these terms: a founder can be considerably more prescriptive in advance than a settlor usually is, and correspondingly less free to adjust once the documents are signed. Families who know exactly what they want tend to prefer the first position. Families whose circumstances are still moving tend to need the second.

What each does at the point the family disagrees

In a trust, a disagreement tends to reorganise itself into a claim against the trustee, because that is the shape the law makes available. Disclosure of trust documents becomes the first battleground, the trustee may seek directions from the court, and the family's argument acquires a defendant who is not a family member.

In a foundation, there is no proprietary interest for a relative to assert, so a grievance has to be expressed as a proposition about purpose: that the council has acted outside it, or has failed to act in accordance with it. The first forum is usually internal, being the council itself and whatever body the founder created to sit above it.

Neither structure resolves a family disagreement. What each does is decide, years in advance, who has to make the decision anyway and how an objection has to be framed. If the real problem is two siblings who cannot be in a room together, that belongs in the design conversation rather than in the drafting.

Why the family drives the choice, not the tax position

The firm does not provide tax advice and does not structure arrangements by reference to their tax outcome. That analysis stays with you and with counsel wherever the family is resident. The group operates under the automatic exchange of information and reports as required in each jurisdiction where it operates, so reporting is a fixed feature of the design rather than a variable within it.

What remains, once tax sits where it belongs, is the family. Three questions do most of the work. Can the founder state a purpose precisely enough to be written down, or do they need a human being to interpret their wishes for the next thirty years? Is the beneficiary class settled, or will it change in ways nobody can currently name? And is a trust intelligible to this family? A principal from a civil-law background often finds a legal person easier to explain to relatives, and to their own authorities, than an arrangement in which someone else owns what the family regards as theirs.

When neither is the answer

If the founder wants to keep control, neither fits, and saying so in the first meeting is cheaper than discovering it in year three. If the estate is one operating company with one obvious successor and no cross-border element, a shareholders' agreement and a carefully drafted will may do more work than either vehicle. And timing constrains everything: succession structures generally take longer to put in place than the transactions that prompt them, so the work usually begins well before a sale. A client six weeks from signing is rarely a candidate for either.

Where this leaves your relationship with the client

The firm works alongside a client's existing tax and legal advisers rather than replacing them. Northwind Fiduciary AG holds a trustee licence in Liechtenstein and is supervised by the FMA. The firm does not provide investment advice or discretionary portfolio management.

If a client's facts are pointing this way, the conversation that tends to be useful first is about the family rather than the vehicle, and it is one you would sit in on. What comes out of it is a plain description of how each structure would behave in your client's particular disagreement, which you can then test against everything you already know about them.
