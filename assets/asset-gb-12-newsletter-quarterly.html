Why this quarter's note is about governance

newsletter · 464 words · published 2026-08-17 · risk tier T3

We send these notes four times a year and give each one a single subject. This quarter's is governance: who decides what, under which document, and what happens when the people a document names are no longer the people a family actually listens to.

The timing is not accidental. The firm has advised on cross-border structures since 1987, and much of what we are asked to read at the moment was drafted in the 1990s, around a family that existed then — one or two decision-makers, a business not yet sold, children not yet adults. Over thirty years, two things happen quietly. The family changes: more branches, more in-laws, more countries. The document does not. Nobody notices, because nothing has yet required a decision the document handles badly. Then something does — a distribution one branch disagrees with, a death, an offer for the business.

This is partly a consequence of how these vehicles are built. A family foundation is established for a defined purpose set out in its founding documents, which govern how it may act. In practice, a council makes the decisions and the founder's instructions were written down in advance rather than given as circumstances arise. That durability is the point of the thing. It is also why a purpose recorded in 1994 is still the instruction being followed in 2026, whether or not anyone in the current generation has read it end to end.

We should be plain about where this line of thinking does not lead. A review is not a rebuild. Quite often the finding is that the structure still does what it was set up to do, and what has shifted is what the family expects of it. That is a conversation among relatives rather than a drafting problem, and saying so is more useful than proposing an amendment. The opposite failure is equally real: a foundation amended each time a disagreement arises tends to end up with a purpose that no longer says anything in particular.

Some readers will know exactly when their founding documents were last read in full, and by whom. Others will find the honest answer is that nobody currently involved has. That is common rather than negligent, and it is a reasonable place for a conversation to start — with us, with the family's own lawyer, or within the family before either. The firm works alongside a client's existing tax and legal advisers rather than replacing them, which here mostly means reading the documents and describing what they say, while the people who already know the family do the recommending. The firm does not provide tax advice and does not structure arrangements by reference to their tax outcome.
