How to check a Liechtenstein fiduciary before you refer a client to one

blog · 1022 words · published 2026-08-17 · risk tier T3

If you are weighing whether to refer a client to a fiduciary in Liechtenstein, the question in front of you is not really which structure the client needs. It is whether the firm you name will still look like a sound choice in two years, when the work is done and your client is telling you what they made of it. This piece sets out what can be checked about us before an introduction is made, and what each of those checks is actually worth.

Start with the licence, because it is the part someone else can verify

Northwind Fiduciary AG holds a trustee licence in Liechtenstein and is supervised by the FMA. That licence carries entry requirements, continuing supervision and professional conduct obligations, and it is granted under the Liechtenstein Trustee Act. The practical point for a referring adviser is that the status does not depend on us describing it. It can be checked with the supervisor rather than taken from our website.

The licence is held by the Liechtenstein entity, not by the group as a whole. That distinction matters more than it sounds, because groups tend to describe regulatory standing loosely and a careful reader will want to know which company is doing the regulated part. Two other entities sit alongside it. Northwind Trust Services AG is affiliated with a recognised self-regulatory organisation for anti-money-laundering purposes. Northwind Corporate Services Pte. Ltd. is a registered filing agent in Singapore. Neither of those is a trustee licence, and we would not want either read as one. Where the work your client needs is trusteeship, it sits with the Liechtenstein entity.

Size and tenure tell you about continuity, not about quality

The firm has advised on cross-border structures since 1987. The group employs 84 people across Liechtenstein, Switzerland and Singapore.

Neither figure tells you whether a particular file will be handled well. What they tell you is something narrower and, for a referral, more useful. A group of that size is small enough that the person who takes the first meeting is usually still the person answering in year six, and large enough that a file does not stall when someone retires or is ill. The tenure matters for a different reason: a good deal of Liechtenstein foundation work involves deeds drafted decades ago by people who are no longer available to explain them, and institutional memory of how those documents were written is not something a firm can acquire quickly.

Awards are the weakest signal available to you

Directory rankings and award citations measure submissions as much as they measure work. Categories shift, panels change, and a citation from six years ago says little about the team who would sit on your client's file today. This piece cites none, which is a deliberate choice rather than an omission.

If you see an award referenced in any fiduciary's materials, including ours, the questions worth asking are the year, the category and who awarded it. Three other checks tend to be worth more: the entry on the supervisor's register, the name and background of the person who would actually hold the mandate, and a reference from another adviser who has made the same referral and seen how it went.

The perimeter, which is the part that touches your own relationship with the client

Advisers who refer once and then lose the client do not refer twice, so it is worth being explicit about where our work stops.

The firm does not provide investment advice or discretionary portfolio management. The firm does not provide tax advice and does not structure arrangements by reference to their tax outcome. The firm works alongside a client's existing tax and legal advisers rather than replacing them. In practice that means the tax analysis stays with you or with whoever your client already uses, and we ask for it rather than produce it. If a structuring question turns on a domestic tax position, that question comes back to you.

One further point, because it shapes what a client should expect. The group operates under the automatic exchange of information and reports as required in each jurisdiction where it operates. A client hoping for something other than that is not a client we can help, and it is easier for everyone if that surfaces before the introduction rather than after it.

What Liechtenstein foundation practice looks like from a common-law seat

A Liechtenstein foundation separates legal ownership of assets from the family that benefits from them. A family foundation is established for a defined purpose set out in its founding documents, which govern how it may act. A trust does something comparable by a different route: a professional trustee holds and administers trust assets for the beneficiaries under the terms of the trust deed.

The difference that most often catches out advisers trained in a trust jurisdiction is where the discretion lives. The founding documents of a foundation do much of the work that a trust deed and a letter of wishes do together, and they are harder to change afterwards. That is the point of them, and it is also the reason the vehicle is the wrong answer for some families. A principal who wants to retain effective control and revisit the arrangement whenever circumstances shift will find a foundation an uncomfortable fit, and is better served by saying so at the outset than by discovering it three years in.

Timing is the other thing worth flagging to a client early. Succession structures generally take longer to put in place than the transactions that prompt them, so the work usually begins well before a sale.

If you are weighing a referral

The reasonable next step is not a meeting. It is a short call with the person who would hold the mandate, on a specific file, to hear how they describe the constraints rather than the possibilities. Advisers who make that call tend to know within twenty minutes whether the referral is one they want to put their name to.
