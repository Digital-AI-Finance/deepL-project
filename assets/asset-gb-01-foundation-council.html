What a Foundation Council Is Bound By

blog · 1052 words · published 2026-08-17 · risk tier T3

A second- or third-generation principal rarely meets a foundation at the moment it is created. The document was signed by a grandparent, or by a parent acting on advice from people who have since retired. What arrives with the inheritance is not a plan but an institution, already running, with a body of people making decisions about assets the family regards as its own. The reasonable first question is not whether a foundation is a good idea. It is who decides, and what they are allowed to decide.

That body is the foundation council. Most of the friction families experience with an older structure comes from a misunderstanding of what the council is for, so it is worth setting out plainly.

The council answers to the purpose, not to the family

A Liechtenstein foundation separates legal ownership of assets from the family that benefits from them. The assets belong to the foundation. The family's relationship to them runs through whatever the founding documents say it does, and not through ordinary ownership.

A family foundation is established for a defined purpose set out in its founding documents, which govern how it may act. The council's authority comes from those documents and is limited by them. When a council considers a request, the question it is required to work through is what the founder set down, not what the family would prefer this year.

This is the part that surprises people who have spent a lifetime running their own affairs. A council that simply did what the family asked would not be a council. It would be an administrative function with no independent standing, and the structure would not be doing the thing it was built to do.

What the council actually does

In practice the work is unglamorous and continuous. The council holds and administers the foundation's assets, keeps records of its decisions and the reasoning behind them, considers requests from beneficiaries against the stated purpose, appoints and instructs external advisers, and meets the reporting and filing obligations that apply to the foundation.

Those obligations are real and ongoing. The group operates under the automatic exchange of information and reports as required in each jurisdiction where it operates. A council that treated reporting as optional would be failing at the most basic part of its job.

The council also decides nothing about how assets are invested in the sense some families expect. The firm does not provide investment advice or discretionary portfolio management. Where investment decisions are needed, they sit with whoever the family and the council have properly appointed for that role.

The constraint is the point, and it is the main cost

A founder who wanted decisions to follow the family's mood would not have needed a foundation. The reason to put assets into one is usually the opposite: to fix a purpose in place so that it survives disagreements the founder could see coming, or could not see but expected. Insulating decisions from whoever happens to be most forceful in a given decade is the service the structure performs.

The cost arrives later, and it is the same feature seen from the other side. A family that has changed cannot simply instruct the council to change with it. A branch that has moved abroad, a business that has been sold, a generation with different needs from the one the founder had in mind — none of these, on their own, alter what the documents say. Councils that bend to accommodate them are not being helpful. They are acting outside their mandate, and that creates a problem that outlasts the accommodation.

It is worth being honest that this makes a foundation the wrong answer for some families. If what you want is flexible control that tracks the family's current thinking, a structure whose defining feature is resistance to current thinking will frustrate you every year you hold it.

When circumstances outgrow the purpose

When the gap between the documents and the family becomes obvious, there are usually more options than families assume, and fewer than they hope.

The first step is almost always to read what already exists, properly. Founding documents drafted decades ago are frequently broader than the family's working memory of them. Purposes are often stated in general terms; by-laws and letters of wishes may address situations nobody remembers discussing; supervisory or protector roles may exist on paper and have fallen into disuse. A significant proportion of apparent deadlocks turn out to be a documentation question rather than a structural one.

Where the documents genuinely do not reach the family's situation, the route to change depends on what the founder reserved and what Liechtenstein law permits for that foundation. Some changes can be made by the organs of the foundation itself. Others require formal steps and, in certain cases, involvement of an authority or a court. None of them are quick, and none of them should begin with a decision about the outcome.

Sometimes the conclusion is that the structure has done its work and the discussion is about bringing it to an orderly end rather than reshaping it. That is a legitimate answer, and it takes longer than families expect.

Reading what you already have

A review that is worth doing looks at three things together: what the founding documents actually say, what the council has actually been doing, and how the family actually operates now. The distance between the three is the finding. Looking at the legal form alone tends to produce advice that is technically correct and useless.

The firm works alongside a client's existing tax and legal advisers rather than replacing them. The firm does not provide tax advice and does not structure arrangements by reference to their tax outcome. Where a family already has counsel who know its history, that history is usually the most valuable input available, and displacing it would be a poor trade.

If you are not certain what your foundation's stated purpose is, or who currently sits on its council and under what authority, those are answerable questions and the answers are on paper. Reading them before the next family conversation, rather than during it, tends to make that conversation shorter.
