Why succession structuring starts before the sale process does

blog · 936 words · published 2026-08-17 · risk tier T3

Most owner-managers start thinking seriously about ownership and succession when a buyer appears. By then the part of that work which changes anything is largely behind them.

Succession structures generally take longer to put in place than the transactions that prompt them, so the work usually begins well before a sale. That is not a scheduling preference; it follows from how differently the two processes run. A sale is negotiated against a deadline someone else sets. A structure has to be explained to a family, agreed with co-shareholders, accepted by a bank, and still make sense to whoever inherits it in twenty years.

If a sale or partial sale is one to three years away, this is the window in which choices are still cheap to change.

Sequence matters more than the vehicle

The first question most people ask is whether they need a trust or a foundation. It is the wrong first question, because the two do similar work by different means, and the choice between them depends on answers most owners have not settled yet.

Briefly, so the terms are not a mystery: a professional trustee holds and administers trust assets for the beneficiaries under the terms of the trust deed. A Liechtenstein foundation separates legal ownership of assets from the family that benefits from them, and a family foundation is established for a defined purpose set out in its founding documents, which govern how it may act.

Both can hold shares in an operating company. Neither will fix a disagreement about who was told what.

The questions that actually determine the answer come earlier:

  • Who holds the shares today, and did that come about by design or by accretion over twenty years?
  • Which family members expect to receive something, and have they been told?
  • Is anyone in the next generation going to work in the business, or has that already been quietly settled?
  • If you or a child moved country within five years, which of today's arrangements would have to be unwound?

Work through those and the vehicle tends to select itself. Choose the vehicle first and the following year is spent bending facts to fit it.

What signing changes

A signed sale agreement converts a hard-to-value private holding into a number, and it does so on a fixed date. Several things become harder at that point.

Moving shares mid-process is visible. Once a buyer's advisers are in the room, a transfer of ownership becomes part of the disclosure exercise, may require consents from co-shareholders or lenders, and invites questions about why it is happening now. Whatever the answer, it costs negotiating attention at the moment there is least of it to spare.

Conversations that have not happened become conversations that cannot happen calmly. Telling a sibling or an adult child about an intended arrangement is a different discussion before a price exists than after one does.

And after completion the proceeds usually sit in one name, in one jurisdiction, subject to whatever succession rules apply there, which is the position many owners set out to avoid in the first place.

None of this is about the tax treatment of anything. It is about consent, disclosure and reversibility.

What can still be done in the last six months

More than people assume, as long as expectations are set honestly.

Entities can be formed relatively quickly. Governance can be written down: who decides, on what, and what happens when they disagree. Documents can be gathered, including shareholder registers, old agreements and previous wills, which is dull work and frequently uncovers a problem nobody knew about. Advisers can be appointed and briefed so that the transaction team and the succession team are not learning about each other during the closing week. Where proceeds will be received, and by which entity, can be decided.

What does not fit into six months is anything that depends on a fact having been true for a period of time, anything requiring a change of personal residence, and anything that needs a family member to come round to an idea they first heard about last month. Those have their own timetables and are not compressible by paying more.

When the answer is no new structure

Sometimes there is nothing to build. If the business is essentially the whole estate, the family is one generation, and there is a single intended beneficiary who already works alongside the owner, a foundation may add administration without answering a question anyone is asking. A shareholders' agreement, a current will and a direct conversation may do the work instead. A structure that exists because it was available rather than because it was needed becomes a maintenance obligation for the people who inherit it.

Where this leaves you

If a transaction is somewhere on the horizon, the useful next step is not choosing a vehicle. It is writing down the facts, including ownership, expectations, residence and who has been told what, and putting that document in front of your own lawyer and accountant before anyone drafts anything.

The firm works alongside a client's existing tax and legal advisers rather than replacing them. It does not provide tax advice and does not structure arrangements by reference to their tax outcome, and it does not provide investment advice or discretionary portfolio management. A corporate lawyer who has not handled cross-border succession before is a common starting point rather than a shortcoming, and it is usually the moment at which a second conversation earns its keep.
