Trusts, foundations, and why the order of the work matters more than the vehicle

blog · 1068 words · published 2026-08-17 · risk tier T3

Most owner-managers who talk to us one to three years before a sale open with the same question: trust or foundation? It is a fair question, and it is usually the second one. The first is what has to happen in what order — because the order determines whether the answer to the second question is still available when you need it.

What a trust does

A professional trustee holds and administers trust assets for the beneficiaries under the terms of the trust deed. Legal title moves to the trustee, who is bound by a document you write and who can be held to it by the beneficiaries and by a court. A trust is not a company: it has no shares and no register of members, and it exists as a set of obligations rather than as a thing.

The practical consequence for someone still running a business is worth stating plainly. Once shares are held in trust, you are not their legal owner. You may still sit on the board and still run the company day to day, but the ownership decision has been taken, and reversing it is slower and more conspicuous than taking it was.

What a foundation does

A Liechtenstein foundation separates legal ownership of assets from the family that benefits from them. It differs from a trust in one structural respect that tends to matter more than the rest: a foundation is a legal person in its own right. It holds assets as itself, it can be a shareholder, and it appears on a register under its own name.

A family foundation is established for a defined purpose set out in its founding documents, which govern how it may act. The purpose is written at the beginning, by the founder, and the foundation council is bound by it afterwards. Families who favour foundations usually favour exactly this: the founder's intentions survive in a document rather than in the memory of whoever happened to be in the room. Families who dislike foundations dislike the same feature described the other way round — it is harder to change your mind later.

Neither vehicle is better than the other in the abstract. They fail differently, and which failure a family can live with depends on the family, not on the law.

Why the order matters more than the vehicle

Succession structures generally take longer to put in place than the transactions that prompt them, so the work usually begins well before a sale. That is the whole argument for starting early, and it is a practical argument rather than a financial one.

Consider what a sale process does to the timetable. Once a buyer is in diligence, every change to the ownership of the target becomes a disclosure item, a warranty question and a delay. Once heads of terms are signed, the shareholders who will sign the sale agreement are effectively fixed. Moving shares into a structure after that point is not impossible, but it happens on someone else's timetable, with a valuation already anchored and with a counterparty entitled to ask why.

Doing the same work eighteen months earlier is the same work. It is simply done when the only deadline is yours.

Where neither structure is the right answer

Sometimes the honest answer is that no vehicle is needed. If the business will be sold outright, the proceeds are going to two adult children who agree with each other, and nobody is moving jurisdiction, then a will and a shareholders' agreement may do most of what a structure would do, at a fraction of the running cost and with far fewer people to keep informed.

Structures earn their keep where there is genuine complexity: assets or family members in more than one country, a business that will not be sold in full, a generation that is not ready, or a purpose that has to outlast the founder. Where that complexity is absent, a structure is an ongoing administrative obligation in search of a problem.

What starting now actually buys

Not a number. We cannot tell you that a trust or a foundation will leave your family financially better off than the alternatives, and any firm that puts a figure on that is describing something it does not control. The firm does not provide tax advice and does not structure arrangements by reference to their tax outcome. The firm does not provide investment advice or discretionary portfolio management. The group operates under the automatic exchange of information and reports as required in each jurisdiction where it operates.

What starting now buys is optionality. Decisions taken calmly, in the right order, before a buyer's timetable applies, tend to leave more doors open than the same decisions taken in the last quarter before signing. That is the case for acting early, and it is the only one we would make.

What to ask any firm you speak to

We would rather set out the questions than make claims about the alternatives. Ask who is licensed to do what, and in which jurisdiction. Ask whether the firm will work with the lawyer and accountant you already have, or expects to replace them. Ask what a structure costs to run every year, not only to establish. Ask what happens when the family disagrees, because at some point it will.

For our part: Northwind Fiduciary AG holds a trustee licence in Liechtenstein and is supervised by the FMA. The firm has advised on cross-border structures since 1987. The group employs 84 people across Liechtenstein, Switzerland and Singapore. The firm works alongside a client's existing tax and legal advisers rather than replacing them. Those are facts about us. They are not an argument that we are the right firm for your family, which is a judgement only you and your advisers can make.

If a sale is one to three years out, the most useful next step is usually not choosing a vehicle. It is mapping who owns what today, where everyone concerned is resident, and which of those facts will have changed by completion. That map is what makes the trust-or-foundation conversation a short one. Your own lawyer and accountant can begin it, and we are content to be brought in at the point where the cross-border edges appear.
