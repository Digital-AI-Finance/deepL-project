Automatic exchange of information and the structure your family already has

blog · 1027 words · published 2026-08-17 · risk tier T3

Many family structures still running today were set up before automatic exchange of information existed. The foundation or the trust was created for reasons that had to do with a death, a business, a divorce, or a generation spread across three countries. The reporting regime arrived afterwards, the structure kept running, and in a good number of cases nobody has gone back to ask whether it still fits the family as it now is.

That is the useful question. Not whether information is reported — it is — but whether the arrangement still does the work it was built to do.

What is reported, and to whom

Under the automatic exchange of information, banks and other financial institutions identify who holds an account and, where an entity holds it, the individuals treated as controlling or benefiting from it. They pass that identification, together with balances and certain payments, to their own national authority, which sends it on to the tax authority of each country where those individuals are resident. It happens annually, on a fixed cycle, and it does not wait for anyone to ask.

For a family holding assets through a foundation or a trust, this usually means the entity is identified as the account holder, and named individuals connected to it are identified alongside. Where a beneficiary lives matters more than where the structure sits. A grandchild who moves to another country changes what is reported and where, without anything in the founding documents changing at all.

As a fiduciary group, we are inside this system rather than beside it. The group operates under the automatic exchange of information and reports as required in each jurisdiction where it operates. Northwind Fiduciary AG holds a trustee licence in Liechtenstein and is supervised by the FMA, and that supervision comes with reporting obligations of its own.

Confidentiality, said plainly

We cannot offer confidentiality beyond the ordinary professional duties that bind any fiduciary, and we do not. Those duties are real — they govern how we handle your affairs, who we speak to, and what we do with what you tell us — but they stop where a reporting obligation begins. Anyone describing something broader than that is describing something that no longer exists.

This matters for a specific reason. If a structure was originally built on an assumption about who would see what, that assumption is now obsolete, and the structure needs a different justification. Sometimes there is a very good one. Sometimes there is not.

What the structure is actually for

The justification that survives is governance and succession. A Liechtenstein foundation separates legal ownership of assets from the family that benefits from them. That separation is not about who can see the assets; it is about who decides, on what basis, and what happens when the person who used to decide is no longer there.

A family foundation is established for a defined purpose set out in its founding documents, which govern how it may act. That purpose is the closest thing a family has to a written constitution. If it was drafted for a family of four in one country and the family is now nineteen people in five, the document is still governing — it is simply governing a family that no longer exists in that form.

The same logic applies on the trust side. A professional trustee holds and administers trust assets for the beneficiaries under the terms of the trust deed. The deed decides what the trustee may do. A deed written for an operating company that was sold in 2009 will not read sensibly against a portfolio of properties and a private equity commitment.

None of this is a matter of legal form alone. Structures fail in practice not because the drafting was poor but because the family stopped agreeing on what it was for, and the document had no way of noticing.

When the honest answer is to simplify

A structure is the wrong answer more often than the industry admits. If the assets sit in one country, the next generation intends to hold them directly, and there is no dispute about who receives what, an additional layer adds administration and cost without adding governance. Some families come to us expecting a new vehicle and leave with a shorter list of entities than they arrived with.

It is also worth being direct about what is not driving this analysis. The firm does not provide tax advice and does not structure arrangements by reference to their tax outcome. The firm does not provide investment advice or discretionary portfolio management. What we can do is describe how a structure behaves, and what it will require of the people who inherit it.

Where to start with a structure you inherited

Four questions tend to be more revealing than a full review, and you can put them to your existing documents without anyone's help.

Does the stated purpose describe the family as it is today, including the branches that were not born when it was written? Who has the power to appoint and remove the people who decide — and is that person still alive and still willing? What happens, in the documents, when a beneficiary changes country of residence? And when was the letter of wishes last revised, by whom, and did anyone who now benefits from the structure ever read it?

If the answers are uncomfortable, that is information, not a crisis. Structures of this age are usually amendable, and the work of bringing one back into line with the family is slower and less dramatic than replacing it.

Most families in this position benefit from having the conversation with their own lawyer first, because the family history is the harder half of the problem and their lawyer already knows it. The firm works alongside a client's existing tax and legal advisers rather than replacing them, and the firm has advised on cross-border structures since 1987, which mostly means we have seen what happens when the review is postponed one more generation.
