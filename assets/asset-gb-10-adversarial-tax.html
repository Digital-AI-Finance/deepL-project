A Liechtenstein foundation before a sale: what it does, and what it does not do

blog · 1066 words · published 2026-08-17 · risk tier T3

Owner-managers a year or two from a sale usually arrive with a version of the same question: will putting a structure in place now leave the family better off once everything has been settled with the revenue authorities? It is a reasonable question. It is also the one question we are not the right people to answer, and it is worth saying so before saying anything else.

Northwind Fiduciary AG does not provide tax advice and does not structure arrangements by reference to their tax outcome. That is a perimeter rather than a disclaimer. Where a family ends up depends on where its members are resident, on what the buyer is actually buying, and on when each step happens — questions for the accountant and the corporate lawyer who already know the business. The firm works alongside a client's existing tax and legal advisers rather than replacing them.

What we can explain is the mechanism: what a Liechtenstein foundation is, what it changes, and where the word "efficiency" survives contact with the detail.

What a foundation is, mechanically

A Liechtenstein foundation separates legal ownership of assets from the family that benefits from them. The founder transfers assets to the foundation, and the foundation owns them. It has no shareholders and no members, so there are no shares in it to inherit when someone dies.

A family foundation is established for a defined purpose set out in its founding documents, which govern how it may act. That purpose is written by the founder at the outset, and the useful version of it is specific rather than abstract: which branch of the family benefits, on what conditions, what happens if the operating business is sold, what happens if a child joins the business and a sibling does not. A foundation council then administers the foundation within those documents. It is not free to improvise, and the founder is not free to change its mind for it on a Tuesday afternoon.

That last point is usually harder for an owner-manager to accept than anything on the tax side. Assets held by a foundation are no longer personally owned. The trade is real, and it is the whole reason the structure does anything at all.

Where the word "efficiency" holds up

Used carefully, efficiency in this context describes administration and decision-making. Three concrete versions of it:

One owner instead of six. A family that holds an operating company, two properties and a couple of intermediate holding entities across three countries has signatories, minutes and filing obligations in each. Consolidating ownership under one entity reduces the number of places a single decision has to be made and recorded.

Continuity that does not wait for a probate court. Because the foundation owns the assets and does not die, a death in the family does not suspend the ownership of the business while an estate is administered separately in each jurisdiction where an asset happens to sit.

Terms agreed before the pressure arrives. Provisions written while the founder is well and the sale is still hypothetical tend to be clearer than provisions negotiated among siblings after neither of those things is true.

None of that is a financial outcome. It is a reduction in friction, and friction is what usually costs a family money and goodwill after a sale.

Where a foundation is the wrong answer

If the intention is to keep personal, day-to-day control of the assets, a foundation is a poor fit. There are structures that gesture at both, and they tend to be the ones that are challenged later.

If the family and all of the assets sit in one jurisdiction, a will, a shareholders' agreement and a clear conversation may do the same work with less machinery.

And if completion is ten weeks away, restructuring ownership in the middle of a transaction usually creates more problems than it solves. Leaving the shareholding alone until after closing, and dealing with the proceeds afterwards, is often the better sequence.

Why the sequence matters more than the vehicle

Succession structures generally take longer to put in place than the transactions that prompt them, so the work usually begins well before a sale.

The reason is not administrative slowness. It is that some steps cannot be unwound. Transferring shares, changing personal residence and signing heads of terms all change the position, and they change it in an order that matters. A decision that looks neutral eighteen months out can remove an option that would otherwise still have been open at completion. This is why the first useful conversation is about the timetable, not about whether a trust or a foundation is preferable in the abstract.

Trust or foundation

A professional trustee holds and administers trust assets for the beneficiaries under the terms of the trust deed. A foundation is different in form: it is a legal person in its own right, with its own council, rather than a relationship between a trustee and beneficiaries.

Which form fits depends far less on the features of the vehicle than on where the family members live, how their home jurisdictions treat each form, and how the family actually makes decisions. That is a question for your own advisers, informed by an accurate description of both — which is the part we can supply.
